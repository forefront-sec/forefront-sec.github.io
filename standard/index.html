The Evidence-Grade Report Standard

Version 1 · published November 2026 · maintained by Forefront Security

Most pentest reports are prose with screenshots. An evidence-grade report is built so your auditor — or your own engineer — can verify every finding without taking the vendor's word for it. This is our published internal standard: the six things we require of every report we deliver. It is free to adopt; we ask only that you cite it.

Why this standard exists

A pentest report has one job: let someone who wasn't in the room verify what was found, act on it, and prove the fix. Most reports fail that job the same way — a finding with no raw evidence, a severity with no rationale, a fix with no way to check it, and a "we tested everything" claim with no record of what was actually tested. This standard lists what we require of our own reports. We publish it so buyers can ask every vendor the same six questions.

The six elements

1 · Raw evidence per finding

Every finding cites the server's actual response — captured, not summarized. Byte count and UTC timestamp are recorded at capture time, so the reader can trace the finding to a specific response, not a description of one.

Ask a vendor: "Show me the raw response for one finding, with its byte count and timestamp."

2 · Human verification, stated

Every finding is confirmed by a person, and the report says so. Automated candidates that were not confirmed are not presented as findings.

Ask a vendor: "Which findings came from an automated tool, and who verified each one?"

3 · A runnable validator per finding

Each finding ships with a small, non-destructive script that returns a fixed result: 0 = fixed, 1 = still present, 2 = the test rig itself failed. You can run it yourself; the provider's re-run is the attestation.

Ask a vendor: "Can I run a check myself that tells me whether this finding is fixed?"

4 · A scope & coverage statement derived from the evidence

The report states which test areas were performed, which were out of scope, and which were not performed — built from what was actually run in that engagement, not from a generic capability list. A completed engagement is not a security guarantee, and the report says so.

Ask a vendor: "Which test areas did you actually perform in this engagement, and which did you not?"

5 · A claim manifest

Each finding's expected state before and after the fix is recorded in a machine-readable manifest, with its evidence reference. Claims and evidence are bound together.

Ask a vendor: "Can you give me a manifest that links each claim to its evidence?"

6 · An attestation

The report is signed, dated, and tied to the evidence it cites. The signature identifies who reviewed and approved the delivery.

Ask a vendor: "Who signed this report, and what exactly did they attest to?"

What this standard is not

  • Not a certification. Adopting it does not mean a provider is "certified" or "compliant" with anything.
  • Not a guarantee of security. A report that meets this standard documents what was tested and found — it does not mean all vulnerabilities were found or that a system is secure.
  • Not a framework-alignment claim. This standard defines report evidence quality; it does not claim alignment with any testing framework. Any framework-mapped coverage is a separate, per-engagement artifact.
  • Not a template to copy verbatim. The value is in the practice — evidence captured at the source, validators that actually run, an annex built from the ledger.

How to use it

Buyers

Copy the six "Ask a vendor" questions into your RFP or vendor evaluation.

Auditors

Use the six elements as a checklist for whether a submitted report is evidence-grade.

Providers

Adopt it, cite it, improve it. If an element is missing, open a conversation — this is v1.

Our position

We build our reports to this standard, and every engagement ships these six elements. We publish it because buyers should be able to compare — and because a market where reports are verifiable is better for everyone in it.
