For auditors: how this report fits your evidence request

A pentest report is one input to your examination, not a control opinion. This page shows how an evidence-grade report answers the tests auditors apply to vendor evidence — and, plainly, what it does not prove. Your auditor sets the requirement and applies the criteria; this page is general information, not compliance advice.

The five tests auditors apply to evidence

Whatever the framework, examiners weigh evidence on the same five dimensions. Here is how a report built to the Evidence-Grade Report Standard answers each.

Authenticity — is this the record the provider actually produced?

Every finding cites the raw server response, byte-counted and UTC-stamped at capture. The report is signed and dated; its evidence index is hashed. You can trace each claim to a specific capture, not a summary of one.

Relevance — does it speak to the period and the system in scope?

The signed ROE names the targets, the test window, and the method. The report states what was in scope, what was out of scope, and what was not performed — derived from the evidence, not a generic capability list.

Completeness — is anything material omitted or over-read?

Findings are separated from hardening notes; disproven hypotheses are recorded alongside confirmed ones. A scope & limitations statement makes over-reading the result hard by design.

Sufficiency — is it enough to support a conclusion?

Each finding carries an attack path, reproduction steps, and a runnable validator (exit 0 = fixed). The retest ledger shows status over time. Whether that is sufficient for your file is your professional judgment — the report is built to make that judgment possible.

Timeliness — does it cover the period you opine on?

A pentest is point-in-time. For a period-based examination, the dated report plus the retest ledger show activity across the window. The report says plainly that it is a point-in-time assessment, not continuous monitoring.

Where it fits in SOC 2

SOC 2 evaluates controls against the AICPA Trust Services Criteria: five categories (Security, Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality, Privacy), with the Common Criteria (CC1–CC9) as the required Security set. Penetration testing is not a single named control — it is evidence that supports several, and the AICPA's own guidance lists penetration testing among the evaluations management may use in its monitoring activities.

Because the specific control mapping depends on your system description and the criteria you scope, we do not publish a fixed control-by-control crosswalk. The report is structured so you can place each finding against the criteria you apply; we will provide a mapping to the criteria you name on request, as a per-engagement artifact — not a compliance claim.

Framework facts above are drawn from the AICPA 2017 Trust Services Criteria structure and public auditor guidance. We do not reproduce or paraphrase the criteria text, and nothing here is an audit opinion.

What this report proves — and does not prove

Proves
These specific weaknesses were present in the tested system on the stated dates; each is reproducible from the cited evidence; the fixes were (or were not) verified on retest.
Does not prove
That no other weaknesses exist; that controls operate across the full period; that the tester is competent or independent beyond what is stated; or that remediation is complete beyond what was retested.

Independence and review — stated plainly

Forefront is a solo practice. The analyst who performs the testing also signs the report; that is a self-review, and the report says so in its attestation rather than implying a second reviewer exists. If your examination requires independent review of the tester's work, tell us at scoping — we will say whether we can meet it, rather than overstate what we provide. Model choice and version are recorded in every engagement log and available to you.
